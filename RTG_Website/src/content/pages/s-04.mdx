## The problem, as it usually gets described to us

A seismic spread stays in the water for weeks. The deck equipment that put it there
runs continuously — not in the sense that it is always turning, but in the sense
that it is always on duty, always the thing standing between a full spread and a
very expensive afternoon. Bearings, seals, brakes and hydraulic fluid all accumulate
hours on a seismic vessel faster than anywhere else in the survey fleet.

And there is no window to attend to any of it. A hydrographic vessel can end a line,
deal with a problem and pick up where it left off. With a spread deployed, you
cannot. Recovery is not an interruption, it is a day — sometimes more if the weather
has an opinion.

Which means the question about a seismic winch is never what it can lift. It is how
long it will run without asking for anything, and what can be reached and serviced
while the gear is still in the water.

## A deck is not one machine

A seismic deck is a dozen machines that have to behave like one. Winches deployed
together, recovered together, holding tension together. One unit that runs slightly
differently from the one beside it is not a maintenance issue — it is a geometry
issue, and geometry is what you are being paid to acquire.

Add the environment: constant salt, constant vibration, gear that gets handled hard
because there is never enough time to handle it gently, and a crew rotation that
means whoever fixes it at 4am may not be whoever installed it.

Nothing here is a dramatic failure mode. That is exactly why it is expensive.
