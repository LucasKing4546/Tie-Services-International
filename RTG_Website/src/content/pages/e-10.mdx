## How it is built

Self-contained, on one common skid. Two-track electro-hydraulic tensioners
from 5 to 20 tonnes, with an integrated hydraulic power unit on the same
frame — the system needs an electrical supply and nothing else. Controlled
from a remote console over a single twenty-metre Ethernet cable, with
rotary controllers, illuminated push buttons and a nine-inch HMI carrying
full alarm and monitoring. Road transportable, craned on, running the same
day — not a vessel integration project.

Tension and squeeze are both measured, not inferred: four load pins
interfaced with calibrated weighing modules on each track. V-grooved
rubber-bonded pads, bolted rather than fixed, so the pad profile can be
matched to the product rather than the other way round.

## Squeeze versus line tension, explained

Two numbers on a tensioner datasheet, sitting next to each other, measuring
completely different things. Confusing them is the most common way to
specify the wrong machine, and one of the more common ways to damage a
cable.

**Line tension** is the load carried along the cable — what the tensioner
holds back against, set by the weight of suspended product between the
vessel and the seabed, the lay angle, the water depth and the dynamics of
the vessel. **Squeeze tension** is the force the tracks apply across the
cable to grip it. It acts at ninety degrees to the line tension and has
nothing to do with the weight being held — it is what stops the product
slipping through the machine.

On the 10-tonne unit, line tension is variable up to 10 tonnes. Squeeze is
variable from 0.5 to 25 tonnes — well beyond the line figure, and
deliberately so. The tracks hold the cable by friction, and friction gives
only a fraction of what is squeezed with: at a coefficient of around 0.2
between pad and cable sheath, holding one tonne of line tension needs
roughly five tonnes of squeeze before the cable begins to slip. That ratio
falls when the sheath is wet, muddy, cold, when the pads are worn, or when
the product is smooth-jacketed rather than textured — conditions that occur
during a normal lay, which is why the squeeze range has to be generous
enough to cover the worst combination actually met at three in the morning
in February.

And the reason it cannot simply be wound up: a cable is not a solid bar.
Underneath the sheath are conductors, insulation, water blocking, armour
and often fibre, and all of it has a permitted crush loading. Exceed it and
the geometry flattens, the water blocking is disturbed, or the insulation
is stressed — and nothing visible happens. The cable emerges from the
tracks the same shape it went in, the lay completes, and the fault appears
when the array is energised or some time after.

Setting the squeeze correctly is a conversation about the product, not the
machine: its permitted crush loading, diameter and construction, the pad
profile and contact area, and the line tension actually needed at the lay
depth. Those are the manufacturer's numbers, not ours — which is why we
would rather discuss it before the vessel sails. What to check on any
tensioner being compared: the squeeze range, not just its maximum. A
machine with a high top figure and a coarse bottom end cannot hold a
light-construction cable gently, and the low end of the range is usually
where a specification is won or lost.

## Fail-safe braking

A brake that needs power to hold is not a brake. It is a machine that will
let go of a cable at the worst possible moment.

These tensioners use fail-safe braked hydraulic motors: the brake is
applied by spring and released by pressure. Under normal running,
hydraulic pressure holds the brake off and the motor controls the product.
Lose pressure — power failure, hose burst, pump failure, emergency stop,
someone pulling the plug — and the springs apply the brake immediately,
without a command and without anything having to work. The default state
is held; everything the system does to run the cable is an active
departure from that. Static parking brake holds at a minimum of 1.8 times
safe working load, because the load at the moment of failure is not the
load on the datasheet — a vessel movement, a snatch, or a surge as the
drive drops out all arrive on top of the static figure.

Consider what is being held at the moment the power goes: suspended cable
between the vessel and the seabed, under tension, with its own weight
pulling it over the side. If the tracks release, the product runs — and a
running cable in a track system cannot be caught by hand, by reflex or by
a good decision. So the question worth asking any supplier is not whether
the brake is reliable. It is: with no power at all, what is the machine
doing? If the answer requires a battery, an accumulator, a UPS or an
operator, it is not fail-safe — it is a system with a backup, which is a
weaker proposition because it depends on a second thing working at the
exact moment the first one did not.

Every one of these machines has its brake holding proven on our own
stands before dispatch, with calibrated equipment and certified test
weights.

## What decides which unit

Not the length of the campaign. Four properties of the product itself:
outside diameter and construction (armoured, unarmoured, bundled); minimum
bend radius, static and dynamic; permitted crush loading, which sets the
squeeze band before anything else does; and required line tension at the
water depth and lay angle actually worked at. Send those four and the
vessel, and we can say which capacity fits and what the track and pad
configuration should be.
