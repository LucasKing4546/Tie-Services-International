## Drive comparison

Hydraulic drives meter flow through a proportional valve. At a few metres a
minute the valve is operating on a very small fraction of its range, the
least linear part of its characteristic, and the available resolution sets
a floor on how finely speed can be commanded. Closed-loop control with
speed feedback compensates for a great deal of this, but it cannot create
resolution the valve does not have.

Electric drives hold low speed by measuring it. A closed-loop drive with
encoder feedback produces full torque at near-zero speed and corrects
continuously, which is why survey and research vessels specify electric so
often — not because electric is better in general, but because it is
better at the slow end, where a CTD cast, a slow tow or a controlled
descent actually happens. On payout it also has to absorb energy rather
than deliver it, so the drive has to be specified to handle a regenerating
load rather than trip on it.

Both drive types are built for the duty rather than for preference, and we
will say which one a cast profile argues for even when it is the less
convenient answer — hydraulic where vessel power is limited or the deck
environment argues for it, electric where precise slow-speed control and a
clean installation matter more.

## Level wind options

At speed, a level wind has momentum on its side and the rope more or less
falls where it should. At survey speeds the rope has to be actively
placed, turn by turn, and every mechanism's backlash shows up as a small
lateral hesitation — most visibly at the reversal, where the level wind
changes direction and the rope steps across the flange. Done cleanly it is
invisible; done with slop it puts a small tension transient into the cable
at every wrap.

Mechanical, electric and hydraulic level winds behave very differently at
two metres a minute, and the difference rarely appears on a datasheet.
Actuation is specified for placement at low speed rather than for
throughput, and on the compact family the spooling device is chain-driven
from the winch's own gearmotor, with the sprocket and chain changed to
match the cable diameter actually being run.

## Applications

Designed for marine and oceanographic survey operations from small
research and survey vessels, where electric operation gives precise,
controlled deployment and a compact footprint keeps deck space that a
larger vessel would not need to think about:

- Side-scan sonar towing
- Magnetometer systems
- Sub-bottom profiler operations
- Water sampler handling
- CTD deployment
- Drop-down camera deployment
