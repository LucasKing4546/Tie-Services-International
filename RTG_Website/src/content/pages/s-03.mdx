## The problem, as it usually gets described to us

The cable is the cheapest part of the project and the most expensive thing that
can go wrong with it. Offshore cables account for less than a tenth of a wind
farm's total capital cost, and yet around 85% of the total value of offshore
wind insurance claims relate to subsea cables. The industry has become good at
building turbines and foundations. It is still losing money on the thing that
connects them.

**Tension control: the damage happens while it looks fine.** On the lay,
tension is the only thing standing between a cable and its own weight. Too
little and the product slips in the tracks or takes a snatch load as the
vessel moves. Too much and you are pulling against the armour, working the
conductors, stressing the very thing that has to survive twenty-five years
underwater. Neither failure announces itself. The cable goes over the side,
the tension trace looks acceptable, the lay completes. What has actually
happened is inside the construction, and nobody will know until it is
energised.

**Bend radius: the mistake that is over in a second.** Minimum bend radius is
not a handling guideline, it is a design limit, and the cable does not
recover from having been outside it. A tight pass over a chute, an overbend
at the tensioner exit, a moment on the quay when the cable was manhandled
because the window was closing: any of those can compromise the armour, the
water blocking or the insulation permanently. The cable comes out the other
side looking exactly the same as it went in.

**And then the array is energised.** Array cable repairs average around 40
days of downtime and export cable repairs around 60, with combined repair and
lost-generation losses running from roughly $1.2m to $12m for array cables
and $10m to $30m for export cables. Locating the fault is its own problem.
Recovering the cable, cutting out the damaged section and jointing it needs a
vessel, a weather window and a crew, none of which are standing by. The
revenue not earned while that happens is usually the larger half of the bill.

An estimated 90% of power cable insurance claims arise from damage caused
during installation. Which means the failure that surfaces in year two was
almost certainly created on the deck in year zero, by a tension excursion
nobody logged, or a bend nobody saw. That is the case for equipment that
controls tension precisely, records what it did, and protects bend radius by
geometry rather than by the vigilance of a tired crew at four in the morning.

## Site investigation

Before anyone drives a monopile, someone has to know what is under it. The
ground model that sizes a foundation comes from boreholes and cone
penetration tests, and those come off a deck, which makes the handling
system a rather more consequential piece of equipment than its share of the
project cost suggests. An incomplete ground model gets resolved in one of two
expensive ways: every foundation across the array is designed conservatively
to cover the uncertainty, or the uncertainty is discovered during
installation.

Wind site investigation is a volume problem, not one deep, careful
deployment. It is dozens of positions across an array, inside a weather
window that will not be extended, often from a vessel chartered for the
season. So the constraints differ from a research campaign at the same
depths: cycle time at each position, reliability across the whole campaign
rather than the day, and repeatability: handling behaviour that varies
between deployments puts scatter into the ground model itself. The vessel is
usually borrowed too, so deck seating, loading and lead-off geometry have to
work on a hull nobody had specified when the equipment was ordered.

What we supply for it: corer, CPT and vibrocorer handling systems, with
constant tension control for the moment the tool goes captive in the seabed
and the vessel does not stop moving; deck seating design and deck loading
calculations delivered with the equipment rather than commissioned
separately; containerised and skid-mounted spreads where the hull changes
between campaigns. The engineering behind it is the same discipline that
took an RTG tension control system to a <Fig of="cptDepthM" /> working depth
record on a CPT sampling system, a considerably harder problem than
anything in the North Sea, applied to a considerably more repetitive one.

## Cable handling

Every array is also a cable project. Inter-array strings, export cables,
interconnectors: all of it has to be paid off a deck at a controlled
tension, without damaging something that costs more per metre than the
machine handling it. Availability is the constraint, not capability.
Complete cable handling packages are quoted with lead times running past two
years, from a supplier base a buyer can count on one hand. Meanwhile the
vessels doing the work are increasingly chartered and converted rather than
purpose-built, because the purpose-built ones are booked.

Two-track electro-hydraulic tensioners, 5 to 20 tonnes, self-contained on one
skid that needs an electrical supply and nothing else. Controlled from a
remote console over a single twenty-metre Ethernet cable, with a nine-inch
HMI carrying full alarm and monitoring. Road transportable, craned on,
running the same day, not a vessel integration project.

Squeeze tension is not line tension, and confusing them ruins cable. Squeeze
is the grip the tracks apply across the product to hold it without slipping.
Line tension is what travels along it. On the 5-tonne unit, line tension is
variable to 5 tonnes while squeeze is variable from 0.5 to 25 tonnes, five
times the line figure, and deliberately so. Too little and the product slips
through the tracks; too much and you crush a cable worth more than the
tensioner. Getting that relationship right for the specific product is most
of the commissioning conversation, and it is worth having before the vessel
sails.

Fail-safe means the brake holds when the power does not: a static parking
brake at a minimum of 1.8 times safe working load, on fail-safe braked
hydraulic motors. On a loss of power the product is held, not released. What
we would want to know: the product (diameter, construction, minimum bend
radius and permitted crush loading), the vessel, and whether it is yours, and
the date, because in this market that is usually the deciding factor.

## Self-contained skids for vessels of opportunity

The purpose-built spread is booked. The work is not waiting. A great deal of
offshore wind gets done from whatever suitable hull is available for the
season (a converted supply vessel, a multi-purpose ship, a barge) and the
equipment has to arrive ready rather than expecting the vessel to have been
designed around it. That is a design brief, not a compromise, and it changes
what gets engineered:

- **One skid, one supply.** The system arrives complete (power unit,
  controls, guarding), needing an electrical supply and nothing else. No
  hydraulic ring main, no vessel integration project, no naval architect in
  the loop before the equipment can be craned aboard.
- **Lifted, not improvised.** Certified lifting lugs and fork pockets
  designed in, with a stated footprint and a twist-lock option.
- **Deck seating and loading supplied with it**, not commissioned separately
  after the charter is signed, the question that most often stalls a
  mobilisation is whether the deck will take the equipment where you want to
  put it, and on a chartered hull nobody has the answer to hand.
- **Controlled from where it makes sense.** A remote console on a single
  Ethernet run means the operating position can go where the sightlines are,
  rather than where the machine happens to sit.
- **Designed to come off again.** Lead-off geometry, footprint and
  interfaces are specified so the same spread transfers to the next hull
  without being re-engineered for it.

What that buys is quay time. Mobilisation is unpaid, and on a chartered
vessel it runs against a clock somebody else is holding. A spread that can be
craned on and commissioned in a shift is a materially different proposition
from one that needs a week alongside, and over a season of moves, that
difference is the whole equipment budget. What we would want to know: the
vessel if you have one, or the constraints if you do not: available deck
area, deck loading, power available, crane capacity, and how many times it
has to move this year.

## Lead times

The reason you are reading this page is probably a date. Complete cable
handling packages are being quoted with lead times running past two years,
from a supplier base you can count on one hand, not a criticism of those
suppliers, it is what a full order book looks like when demand grows faster
than capacity. For a project with a cable campaign inside two years, the
specification conversation and the availability conversation are the same
conversation.

Fabrication and assembly capacity is the part we control, and we publish it
quarterly. What we do not control, and what usually sets the outer bound, is
long-lead bought-in content: drives, control hardware and anything specified
to a particular manufacturer. If a programme is tight, tell us early and we
will say which parts of the specification are costing weeks.

If there is existing equipment in the fleet, rebuilding it is almost always
faster than building new, and sometimes it is the only answer that meets the
date. We we will assess it honestly, including the cases where the answer is
that it is not worth doing.

We quote a date with the assumptions written down. A lead time that depends
on a drawing approval not yet given, or a component not yet confirmed, is not
a lead time: it is an optimistic guess that becomes an argument later. We
would rather set out what has to happen and when, so both sides can see
where the float is.
