## Availability is the specification

A thruster fault can often be worked around between dives. A winch or umbilical
fault stops the campaign, and in the worst case it does not stop there — it takes
the vehicle with it.

Availability is not only about failure, either. A launch and recovery system that
only works in benign conditions quietly removes days from every campaign, and launch
and recovery is the phase in which most vehicle damage happens.

So the question worth asking about a handling system is not what it costs. It is how
many days it gives back — and what one of yours is worth.

## Handling people is a different design problem

Not a heavier version of the same one. Personnel systems carry substantially higher
design factors, redundant braking rather than a single brake with a margin,
fail-safe behaviour on loss of power, and a defined recovery path that still works
when the primary drive does not.

We build these to the framework the operator and their class society require, and we
state which one on every project rather than in general terms.

## Why we do not supply the slip ring

It looks like a gap in the scope. It is the opposite.

The slip ring is where the winch joins your data and power architecture — fibre
count, conductors, voltage, protocol, tether termination. All of that is set by your
vehicle, not by our drum. A supplier who picks it for you is guessing, and the guess
does not show up in the factory. It shows up at commissioning, or on the first dive.

A failing slip ring is also one of the few faults that costs you days twice:
intermittent video and telemetry look like a vehicle problem, so the search starts in
the wrong place.

So the winch is designed to accept the unit you specify. We will procure it, fit it
and function-test it with the machine, so you still take delivery of one tested
assembly from one supplier — we just do not decide which slip ring your ROV should
be talking through.
