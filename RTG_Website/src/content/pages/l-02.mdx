faqs:
  - q: "What is the drum and shaft condition?"
    a: "These are the expensive structural elements. Sound drum and shaft, and refurbishment is almost always the better answer. Significant wear or cracking in either and the economics change quickly."
  - q: "What state are the brakes and hydraulics in?"
    a: "Both are renewable. Neither should decide the outcome on its own — but a hydraulic system that has been contaminated for years tells you something about how the machine has been run."
  - q: "Has the capability target moved?"
    a: "If the work you are winning now needs more depth, more load or better control than the machine was built for, an upgrade during rebuild may get you there. Sometimes it will not, and that is a genuine reason to replace."
  - q: "Can you still get spares?"
    a: "Obsolete control systems are the commonest reason a sound machine is uneconomic. If the electronics cannot be supported, the rebuild has to include replacing them — which is often still cheaper than a new machine, but changes the sum."
  - q: "What does class say?"
    a: "Where the equipment sits in its certification cycle affects both cost and timing. A rebuild that coincides with a renewal survey is more efficient than one that does not."
  - q: "How long do you need it for?"
    a: "Three more years and five more years are different decisions. Be honest about the vessel's own remaining life — there is no sense putting a twenty-year machine into a hull with five years left."

<Note tone="warn" heading="When we will tell you not to">
Significant structural damage to the drum or main frame, a machine that has been run contaminated for years and shows it throughout, or a capability gap too large to close by upgrade. In those cases the honest answer is replacement, and we will say so in the assessment report whether or not we are quoting for the replacement.
</Note>
