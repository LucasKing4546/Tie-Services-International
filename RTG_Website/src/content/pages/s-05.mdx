## Why the winch is part of the instrument

A CTD cast is a measurement. The winch is the only part of the system that
decides where and how fast the sensor moves through the thing it is
measuring, which makes it a component of the instrument, not the equipment
that deploys it. Nobody writes it that way in a specification, and the gap
shows up in the data.

**Descent rate is a sampling parameter.** The sensors have response times,
and the conductivity and temperature sensors do not share them. Descend
faster than the slower one can follow and you get thermal lag and salinity
spiking at the thermocline: an artefact produced by how the instrument was
moved, not by the ocean it moved through. Descend at an inconsistent rate
and the artefact varies within the same cast, which is harder to correct
than a consistent error.

**Instability at low speed reaches the sensor.** At a few metres a minute, a
drive working at the bottom of its range can stick and release rather than
turn smoothly. On deck that is a barely perceptible shudder. On the record
it is periodic noise at a frequency that has nothing to do with the water
column, and it is very difficult to distinguish from signal after the fact.

**Strum is a measurement problem, not a cable problem.** Vortex-induced
vibration on the wire travels down it. For a towed body it also changes the
attitude and depth of the sensor, which means the position recorded and the
position sampled are different.

**Wire out is data.** For anything towed, layback comes from cable deployed,
and a counter that drifts, a measuring wheel that slips, or stretch under
load that nobody accounts for puts error directly into the geo-referencing
of everything acquired. It is discovered in QC, days later, if it is
discovered at all.

**Repeatability is the whole point.** A time series is only a time series if
the casts are comparable. A winch that behaves slightly differently between
deployments, or between operators, introduces variance that looks like
oceanography.

What that means when specifying one: ask what the slowest speed is that it
will hold, with a tolerance attached, not the slowest speed it will turn.
Ask how the level wind behaves through the reversal at the flange, because
at survey speeds it places each turn rather than letting it fall. Ask how
wire out is derived, and from what. None of those questions appear on a
standard datasheet. All three affect the data.

## Slow-speed control

The specification that matters on a research winch is not top speed. It is
turndown: the span between the fastest the machine will run and the
slowest it will hold steadily. A winch doing 90 m/min on the top layer and
required to hold 2 m/min at depth is being asked for better than 45:1, and
to hold it while the load changes: the vessel heaves, wire weight grows on
the way down, and effective drum radius steps at every layer change. Speed
regulation is the ability to hold a commanded rate through all of that, and
it is a different property from having a low minimum setting.

Payout is the harder half. Descending, the load drives the drum and the
drive is resisting rather than pulling: an overhauling load, a different
control problem and often a different quality of result. Speed regulation on
payout specifically is where the cast happens.

**Where the limits actually come from.** Hydraulic drives meter flow
through a proportional valve. At a few metres a minute the valve is
operating on a very small fraction of its range, the least linear part of
its characteristic, and the available resolution sets a floor on how finely
speed can be commanded. Oil temperature moves the behaviour too. Closed-loop
control with speed feedback compensates for a great deal of this, but
cannot create resolution the valve does not have.

Electric drives hold low speed by measuring it. A closed-loop drive with
encoder feedback produces full torque at near-zero speed and corrects
continuously, which is why research vessels specify electric so often, not
because electric is better in general, but because it is better here. On
payout it also has to absorb energy rather than deliver it, so the drive has
to be specified to handle a regenerating load rather than trip on it.

Then mechanical reality sets the real floor: seal and bearing friction
produce a breakaway threshold: the drive builds torque, nothing moves, then
it releases, and gearbox backlash shows up at every direction change. Gear
ratio is a genuine trade: more reduction improves low-speed resolution and
caps top speed. None of that is fixed by the control system, because it sits
upstream of it.

What we do about it: both drive types, chosen for the duty rather than for
preference, and we will say which one a cast profile argues for even when it
is the less convenient answer. Level wind actuation specified for placement
at low speed rather than for throughput. Gear ratios selected against the
working speed, not the transit speed. And it is verified rather than
asserted: every machine runs a speed test on our stands across the layers
before it ships, and the numbers are written into the test document.

## The range

Cable capacities from 300 to <Fig of="cptDepthM" />, electric or hydraulic,
with or without level wind, configured around the cast rather than a
catalogue number.

- **Profiling and CTD winches**, for work where descent rate is a sampling
  parameter and repeatability across casts is the point. Specified for
  controlled slow-speed payout, with level wind actuation chosen for
  placement rather than throughput. Electric drive is the usual answer here,
  though not always. [Survey and oceanographic winches →](/equipment/winches/survey-oceanographic/)
- **Coring and seabed sampling.** Piston, gravity, box and vibrocorer
  handling, with tension control for the moment the tool goes captive in the
  seabed and the vessel does not stop moving. [Geotechnical and coring winches →](/equipment/winches/geotechnical-coring/)
- **Towed bodies and deep tow**, where wire out is a measurement and cable
  protection is the constraint. Lebus grooved drums and controlled level
  wind as standard, because on a towed system the cable is frequently worth
  more than the winch. [ROV, tow and umbilical winches →](/equipment/winches/rov-tow-umbilical/)
- **Launch and recovery.** Stern and side A-frames and mini survey LARS,
  designed around the deck arrangement and the sea state actually worked in
  rather than the one in the brochure. Deck seating design and deck loading
  calculations supplied with the equipment. [Launch and recovery systems →](/equipment/launch-recovery-systems/)
- **Portable and containerised spreads**, for institutions working from
  chartered or shared vessels, or moving a capability between ships in a
  fleet. Certified lifting lugs, fork pockets, a stated footprint and one
  power supply. [Portable and containerised →](/equipment/portable-containerised/)

Three choices shape everything else. **Electric or hydraulic**: electric for
precise slow-speed control and a clean installation, hydraulic where vessel
power is limited, the duty is heavy, or the deck environment argues for it;
we will say which a cast profile points to, including when it is the less
convenient answer. **Level wind, and how it is actuated**: mechanical,
electric or hydraulic behave very differently at two metres a minute, and
the difference rarely appears on a datasheet. **Where the system stops**:
we build the handling; the sensors, terminations and data architecture are
supplied by the institution. We design to accept what has been specified
rather than dictating it, and say clearly where our scope ends: a boundary
drawn honestly at the outset is cheaper than one discovered at
commissioning.

## Institutions we work with

- National oceanographic and marine research institutes: state-funded
  bodies operating research fleets, typically with long procurement cycles
  and demanding documentation requirements
- University marine science departments and their research vessels: often
  operating a single ship, frequently on shared or consortium access, where
  the equipment has to serve many different science parties
- Hydrographic offices: national charting authorities, and the survey
  vessels operating on their behalf
- Fisheries and marine environmental agencies: stock assessment, habitat
  mapping and environmental monitoring programmes
- Polar and deep-ocean research programmes, where the environment sets the
  specification before anything else does
- Ocean observatory and long-term monitoring networks: mooring deployment
  and recovery, where repeatability across years matters more than peak
  capability
- Government research and survey vessel operators, including agencies whose
  work sits between research and regulation
- Vessel operators and managers acting for institutions: the commercial
  crews who run and mobilise science tonnage on someone else's behalf
- Naval hydrographic and oceanographic units, where the science is the same
  and the documentation regime is not
