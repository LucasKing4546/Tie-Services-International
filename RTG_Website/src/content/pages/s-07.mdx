## The problem, as it usually gets described to us

The machine is the easy part. It is everything wrapped around it that decides
whether you can be on the programme.

By the time a requirement reaches a deck equipment supplier it has passed
through a ministry, a prime contractor and a yard, and each of them has added
something. What arrives is not a specification. It is a specification, a
quality standard, a documentation standard, a security schedule and a support
obligation, and none of it is negotiable at the supplier's level even when the
person sending it agrees it is heavy.

Then the configuration freezes. From that point a substitution that would take
a phone call on a commercial job takes a change board and a concession,
because someone has to be able to reconstruct exactly what was fitted, to
which drawing, by whom, in twenty-five years' time when the vessel is halfway
through a refit and nobody involved in the build is still working. And the
vessel will outlive the electronics inside it by two decades — so the
question is not only what is being supplied, but what happens when the drive
specified is discontinued in year eight, and whether there is a plan for it
or an apology.

Meanwhile the questions have stopped being about engineering. Who owns the
supplier. Where the steel comes from. Who has access to the drawings and
where they are stored. Whether the people are cleared. Whether the company
will still be here. None of that appears on a datasheet. It is most of the
assessment.

## Traceability and documentation

Every structural weld we make is already recorded against a weld number, the
marking punch of the welder who made it, the procedure specification followed
and the inspection record. On a government programme, that is where the
requirement starts rather than where it ends.

**Backwards, into the supply chain.** Mill certificates, heat numbers and
cast analysis, held so we can say which material went into which part of
which machine — not which material was on site that month. The same for
bought-in components: manufacturer, batch and origin, recorded at the point
of receipt rather than reconstructed afterwards. Where national content or
export control questions arise, provenance is the only thing that answers
them, and it cannot be assembled retrospectively.

**Forwards, into service.** Equipment serialised at item level, so a specific
machine on a specific hull can be identified and its history followed. That
is what makes an as-built record meaningful twenty years later, when the
vessel is in refit and nobody from the original build is still in post.

**A configuration record, not a certificate pack.** Certificates prove a
moment. A programme needs the state maintained: an as-built list against the
frozen baseline, every deviation and concession recorded with its approval
and its reasoning, and technical publications produced to the standard the
programme specifies rather than to our house format.

**Auditable without our help.** A government quality representative should be
able to arrive, choose an item at random and follow it back to its origin —
material, procedure, welder, inspection, test, concession — from the record
itself. If that only works with one of our engineers standing next to them,
the record is not doing its job.

What we would want to know: which documentation standard applies, which
quality standard governs the work, what technical data rights the programme
requires, and where the surveillance and hold points sit. Those four answers
shape the build from the outset. Discovered late, they are expensive for both
sides.

## Security of supply

A programme asks something a commercial buyer never asks: not whether we will
deliver, but whether we will still be here to support what we delivered, and
whether anything outside our control can stop us.

**Continuity of the business.** We are a company of around eighty people,
privately held, twenty-two years old — a smaller organisation than most
primes are used to placing on a thirty-year hull, and there is no use
pretending otherwise. Against that: the same ownership since 2003, earnings
reinvested into machine tools year after year rather than borrowed against,
and a client relationship that has now run fourteen years across seven
vessels.

**Continuity of capability.** A single facility is a single point of
failure, and we would rather set out how we manage that than claim it does
not exist.

**Continuity of supply chain.** Steel, drives, bearings, hydraulic
components and control hardware all come from somewhere, and each of those
is a route by which somebody else's problem becomes the programme's problem.
We record origin at the point of receipt, hold approved-supplier status
where the programme requires it, and will carry a lifetime buy on nominated
components where obsolescence risk warrants it. Manufacture is inside the
EU, which for European programmes removes a class of customs and origin
exposure that non-EU fabrication carries.

**Sovereignty and control.** Who owns us, where the technical data is held,
who has access to it, and which jurisdictions our supply chain touches are
not administrative questions on a defence programme. We hold our own design
authority and our own drawings, we do not outsource design, and our
technical data sits under our control rather than a third party's.

If a programme's assessment concludes that a company of our size carries too
much single-source risk, we would rather have that conversation early and
openly — including, where it is the right answer, as a subcontractor to a
larger prime who can absorb it.

## Class

On a commercial vessel, class is the authority. On a naval or government
programme it often is not — and knowing which situation applies changes what
gets built, who witnesses it and what the acceptance evidence looks like.

**Naval and defence programmes** are frequently governed by naval ship rules
rather than commercial ones, with the flag state's naval authority holding
final acceptance and a class society acting on its behalf or alongside it.
Requirements around shock, redundancy, survivability, magnetic signature and
electromagnetic compatibility may apply to deck machinery in ways they never
do commercially, and have to be designed in rather than demonstrated
afterwards.

**Government research, survey and coastguard vessels** are usually
commercially classed, with national requirements layered on top — and
personnel handling almost always brings a further regime with it.

nominated society, survey during build, witnessed load testing on our own
stands to <Fig of="loadTestTe" /> with calibrated equipment and certified
test weights, and the full certification dossier issued with the machine.

A government quality representative is not a class surveyor. They are
frequently both present, with different authority, different hold points and
different acceptance criteria. Where a programme applies a NATO AQAP standard
alongside class, the surveillance regime sits over the top of the class
process rather than replacing it, and the inspection and test plan has to
satisfy both.

What we would want to know early: which rule set governs, which society is
nominated, whether a naval authority holds final acceptance, and where the
hold and witness points fall. Those four answers shape the design, the build
sequence and the test programme from the outset. Asked at the start they
cost nothing. Discovered at survey they cost a schedule.
