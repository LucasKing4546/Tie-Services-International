## The range

Deck cranes and lifting equipment for research, survey and offshore vessels,
built to the same welding, traceability and test discipline as everything
else that leaves this factory.

- **Knuckle boom cranes**, for general deck handling where reach has to fold
  away and deck space is at a premium — hydraulic, with the folding geometry
  chosen against the stowed footprint that can actually be spared.
- **Telescopic boom cranes**, where straight reach and outboard capacity
  matter more than compact stowage — over-the-side work, transfers, and
  lifts where the load has to clear a bulwark or a moonpool edge.
- **Provision and utility cranes** — stores handling, container transfer,
  and the everyday lifting a vessel does between campaigns. Unglamorous and
  used more often than anything else on deck.
- **Davits and dedicated lifting arms**, purpose-built for a specific
  recurring lift — a sensor, a basket, a gangway, a specific overboarding
  position.
- **Personnel-rated systems.** Where the lift involves people, the design
  regime changes rather than the margin simply increasing.

**Where we are the right answer.** Usually when the crane is part of a deck
we are already engineering. If we are supplying the A-frame, the winch and
the handling spread, the crane sharing the same deck seating design, the
same loading calculations, the same class submission and the same
certification dossier removes a whole interface — and interfaces are where
schedules slip. If a standalone deck crane and nothing else is needed, there
are specialists who do only that, and we would rather say so.

## Class and proof load

Lifting appliances are the one category where certification is not a
reassurance, it is a condition of use. A crane without a valid test
certificate does not lift.

**Design appraisal first.** Drawings and calculations submitted to the
nominated society before manufacture, so the structure, the mechanism and
the control system are approved as a design rather than assessed after the
fact. Finding out at survey that something needs changing is the expensive
way to do this.

**Survey during build.** Material certification checked against the
approved design, welding witnessed to the approved procedures, and hold
points agreed with the surveyor before we start rather than negotiated as
we go.

**Proof load testing, in our own factory.** Every unit is proof load tested
before it ships, on our own stands, with calibrated measuring equipment and
certified test weights, up to <Fig of="loadTestTe" />. Static proof load to
the required overload factor, then functional testing under load through
the working range — because a structure that passes a static hold can
still bind, interfere or lose control when it is actually moving.

**The certificate and the dossier.** The machine ships with its test
certificate, the test records behind it, material and weld traceability,
and the manuals — not a certificate on its own, but the evidence the
certificate rests on.

**Why testing it here matters.** A crane tested at the factory is tested
somewhere the fix is cheap. The alternative is discovering the problem at
the yard, on the quay, or during commissioning, with a vessel programme
running and a surveyor waiting — and testing on site also means assembling
test weights somewhere they were not planned for, on a deck with its own
loading limits, in weather that gets a vote. We would rather find the fault
in Satu Mare, with the crane in a bay, the weights already certified and a
workshop around it. It is also why we test everything rather than sampling:
the marginal cost of testing a machine in a facility that already exists is
small, and the cost of not having tested it is not.

**Certification at delivery is not certification in service.** We supply
the appraisal, the survey and the proof load certification for the
equipment as delivered. What follows is the operator's: periodic thorough
examination at the interval the regime requires, rope and hook inspection
against discard criteria, and re-certification. Equipment should be
designed so that can actually be done — inspection access to the parts that
need looking at, and documentation that tells a competent person what to
look at and how often. We also re-certify lifting equipment already in
service, including machines we did not build.

## Applications

- Over-the-side deployment — launching and recovering equipment that does
  not warrant a dedicated A-frame: sensors, small tools, sampling gear,
  test equipment. The job a research vessel does constantly and rarely
  specifies for.
- Handling into and out of a moonpool, where the lift has to be positioned
  precisely over an opening rather than simply cleared over a bulwark.
- Container and equipment transfer — moving containerised spreads, spares
  and portable equipment on, off and around a deck. On a vessel running
  containerised systems, this is what the crane actually earns its keep
  doing.
- Storing and mobilising — provisions, consumables and the endless
  handling of a mobilisation, including craning a skid-mounted spread
  aboard at the start of a charter.
- Supporting a handling spread — assisting recovery, positioning a corer
  or tool on deck after it comes over the side, and the rigging work
  around a larger system.
- Deck maintenance and machinery handling — lifting components during
  maintenance, which is also the argument for thinking about crane reach
  when the deck arrangement is designed, not afterwards.
- Personnel and basket handling, where the system is rated for it — a
  different design regime rather than a heavier version of the same one.
